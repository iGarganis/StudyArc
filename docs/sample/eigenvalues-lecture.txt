Lecture 5: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

1. Definition. Let A be a square n by n matrix. A scalar lambda is called an eigenvalue of A if there exists a non-zero vector v such that A v = lambda v. The vector v is called an eigenvector corresponding to lambda. An eigenvector is never the zero vector, but an eigenvalue can be zero.

2. Characteristic polynomial. The relation A v = lambda v can be written as (A - lambda I) v = 0. For this homogeneous system to have a non-zero solution, the matrix A - lambda I must be singular. Therefore det(A - lambda I) = 0. The polynomial p(lambda) = det(A - lambda I) has degree n and its roots are the eigenvalues, so A has at most n distinct eigenvalues.

3. Example. For the symmetric matrix A with rows (2, 1) and (1, 2), det(A - lambda I) = (2 - lambda)^2 - 1, so the eigenvalues are 1 and 3. Solving (A - 3I) v = 0 gives v = (1, 1). For lambda = 1 we get v = (1, -1).

4. Diagonalization. An n by n matrix is diagonalizable if and only if it has n linearly independent eigenvectors. Then A = P D P^-1, where the columns of P are the eigenvectors and D is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues. Powers follow directly: A^k = P D^k P^-1. Distinct eigenvalues guarantee diagonalization. With repeated eigenvalues, the dimension of each eigenspace must be checked. The matrix with rows (1, 1) and (0, 1) is not diagonalizable, because lambda = 1 has algebraic multiplicity 2 but an eigenspace of dimension 1.

5. Trace and determinant. The sum of the eigenvalues equals the trace of A and their product equals the determinant of A. A is invertible if and only if 0 is not an eigenvalue. If lambda is an eigenvalue of an invertible A, then 1/lambda is an eigenvalue of the inverse of A.

6. Symmetric matrices. Real symmetric matrices always have real eigenvalues, and eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal. A rotation matrix by 90 degrees has characteristic polynomial lambda^2 + 1 and eigenvalues plus and minus i, which are not real.
